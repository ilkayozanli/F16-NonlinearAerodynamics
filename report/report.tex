	\section{Introduction}
	
	High-performance fighter aircraft are routinely required to operate at angles of attack far beyond the range where classical, linear aerodynamic theory remains valid. At low angles of attack, the relationship between angle of attack and the resulting aerodynamic forces and moments is smooth and nearly linear, allowing flight dynamics to be analyzed using standard small-perturbation, linear stability theory. However, modern fighters, particularly those designed for high-agility, post-stall maneuvering, deliberately operate in the high-angle-of-attack regime, where this linear assumption breaks down. Flow separation, vortex formation and breakdown, and asymmetric flow effects introduce strong nonlinearities into the aerodynamic forces and moments, which in turn produce nonlinear and sometimes counterintuitive stability and control characteristics.
	
	This project investigates how these nonlinear aerodynamic effects shape the stability and maneuverability of fighter aircraft, using the F-16 as a primary case study due to the extensive open-literature aerodynamic and flight dynamics data available for it. The core aerodynamic dataset originates from NASA Technical Paper 1538 \cite{nguyen1979}, which documents a wind-tunnel-based nonlinear aerodynamic model of the F-16 across an angle-of-attack range of $-20^{\circ}$ to $90^{\circ}$. For the numerical work in this project, this dataset is used in the digitized, simulation-ready form compiled by van Oort and Sonneveldt \cite{vanoort2006}, which reformats the original NASA tables for direct use in MATLAB/Simulink-based flight dynamics models.
	
	
	\section{High Angle-of-Attack Flow Physics}
	
 At low angles of attack, the boundary layer remains attached to the wing's upper surface, and lift increases nearly linearly with angle of attack. As angle of attack increases beyond a critical value, the adverse pressure gradient over the aft portion of the wing becomes too severe for the boundary layer to overcome, and flow separation occurs, initiating near the trailing edge and progressively moving forward as angle of attack continues to increase. This separation reduces the effective lift-curve slope, causing the lift coefficient to grow more slowly, reach a maximum, and ultimately decline --- the classical stall phenomenon \cite{mason2006}.

 Modern fighter configurations, including the F-16, delay and modify this behavior through the use of leading-edge extensions (strakes). Rather than simply generating lift through attached flow, the strake generates a strong, stable leading-edge vortex, which remains attached and continues to generate substantial additional lift well past the angle of attack at which the wing itself has stalled. Wind-tunnel flow-visualization tests on the F-16 configuration showed the outer wing panels stalling near $\alpha = 20^{\circ}$, while the wing-body strake continued producing lift up to a maximum $C_L$ near $\alpha = 35^{\circ}$ \cite[Sec.~``Longitudinal Characteristics,'' Fig.~9]{nguyen1979}.

 At still higher angles of attack, this vortex itself becomes unstable. It undergoes vortex breakdown --- an abrupt disorganization of the previously coherent vortex structure --- beyond which it can no longer sustain the same lift contribution \cite{sedlacek2023}. Because this breakdown process is highly sensitive to small geometric and flow asymmetries, the left and right vortices frequently break down at different angles of attack or in different manners, even when the aircraft is flying with zero sideslip and is otherwise geometrically symmetric \cite{sedlacek2023}. This produces unintended, asymmetric side forces and yawing/rolling moments --- a genuinely nonlinear phenomenon with no counterpart at low angle of attack, and one of the principal drivers of departure and spin susceptibility at high angle of attack \cite{nguyen1979}.
 
 \section{Limitations of Static Aerodynamic Models and the Motivation for Unsteady Modeling}
 
 The aerodynamic data discussed in Section 2, such as the coefficients tabulated in NASA TP-1538, are fundamentally \emph{static}: each force or moment coefficient is represented as a function of the instantaneous angle of attack, sideslip angle, and control surface deflections, with no dependence on the history of the motion. This representation is a reasonable approximation at low angles of attack, where the flow remains attached and adjusts to changes in aircraft attitude almost instantaneously. At high angles of attack, however, the flow is dominated by separated and vortical structures, which do not respond instantaneously to changes in angle of attack. Instead, these flow structures persist, develop, or collapse over a finite time, so that the aerodynamic force or moment at a given instant depends not only on the current angle of attack but also on how rapidly the angle of attack is changing and on the recent history of the motion \cite{iliff1996}.
 
 This behavior is referred to as unsteady aerodynamics, and it means that a purely static lookup-table model becomes increasingly inaccurate as angle of attack increases. Flight-data identification studies on high-angle-of-attack aircraft, such as the parameter identification analysis of the X-29A, address this by explicitly modeling the discrepancy between the static aerodynamic database and actual flight behavior as a state-dependent forcing term, effectively treating unsteady, separation-driven aerodynamic effects above approximately $15^{\circ}$ angle of attack as an additional input to the equations of motion rather than a fixed function of angle of attack alone \cite{iliff1996}.
 
 A related but distinct challenge is that the static coefficients themselves are highly nonlinear functions of angle of attack and sideslip, as shown directly in Figures 9 and 10. Representing such behavior purely as an interpolated lookup table, as in the original wind-tunnel dataset, is functional for simulation but provides little insight into the underlying structure of the nonlinearity and can be cumbersome to update or extend. This has motivated the development of global nonlinear modeling techniques, which fit the tabulated aerodynamic data to compact analytical functions --- for example, multivariate orthogonal polynomial expansions constructed using Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization --- allowing the same nonlinear F-16 aerodynamic characteristics to be expressed in a closed, continuous mathematical form rather than a discrete table \cite{ismail2023}.
 
 Taken together, these developments define the two directions in which
 high-angle-of-attack aerodynamic modelling has moved beyond the static lookup
 table. Global nonlinear function fitting addresses the form of the static
 nonlinearity, replacing a discrete table with a compact continuous
 description. State-dependent formulations such as Equation~\eqref{eq:goman}
 address a more fundamental limitation, namely that the aerodynamic response is
 not a function of the instantaneous flow condition at all. Both are relevant
 to the work that follows: the static tabulated model underpins the equilibrium
 and bifurcation analysis of Sections~5 and~6, while the unsteady state-space
 formulation is implemented separately in Section~7 in order to quantify the
 error that the static assumption introduces once the angle of attack is
 changing rapidly.
 
 The most widely adopted formalisation of these unsteady effects is the
 state-space representation introduced by Goman and Khrabrov \cite{goman1994}.
 Rather than correcting a static table after the fact, this approach augments
 the aerodynamic model with an internal state variable that carries the
 history of the flow. The variable has a direct physical interpretation: it
 represents the streamwise position of the flow separation point on the upper
 surface, normalised so that a value of one corresponds to fully attached flow
 and a value of zero to complete separation. The aerodynamic coefficients are
 then expressed as functions of this internal state rather than of angle of
 attack alone, so that the same instantaneous angle of attack can produce
 different forces and moments depending on the state of the flow that produced
 it.
 
 The internal state is governed by a first-order differential equation of the
 form
 \begin{equation}
 	\tau_{1}\,\dot{x} + x = x_{0}\!\left(\alpha - \tau_{2}\,\dot{\alpha}\right),
 	\label{eq:goman}
 \end{equation}
 where $x$ is the separation point position, $x_{0}(\alpha)$ is its steady
 value as a function of angle of attack, and $\tau_{1}$ and $\tau_{2}$ are time
 constants. The two constants play distinct roles. The relaxation time
 $\tau_{1}$ sets how quickly the separated flow adjusts towards its steady
 configuration after a change in angle of attack, and therefore controls the
 memory of the model. The convection lag $\tau_{2}$ shifts the argument of
 $x_{0}$ so that the separation responds to a slightly delayed angle of attack
 whenever $\alpha$ is changing, which represents the finite time taken for a
 disturbance to convect over the surface. The steady characteristic
 $x_{0}(\alpha)$ is not an additional assumption: it can be recovered directly
 from static wind tunnel data by inverting the Kirchhoff relation for the
 normal force. In steady flight $\dot{x}$ and $\dot{\alpha}$ both vanish, $x$
 equals $x_{0}(\alpha)$, and the formulation reduces exactly to the static
 model, so nothing is lost in the regime where the static tables are already
 valid.
 
 The practical consequence of Equation~\eqref{eq:goman} is aerodynamic
 hysteresis. When the angle of attack oscillates through the stall region, the
 separation point lags behind its steady value, and the coefficient measured
 while the angle of attack is increasing differs from the coefficient at the
 same angle while it is decreasing. A static lookup table traces a single curve
 in the coefficient--angle-of-attack plane and can never reproduce this
 behaviour; the state-space model traces a closed loop whose width depends on
 the rate of the motion. It should be noted that $\tau_{1}$ and $\tau_{2}$ are
 determined from forced-oscillation wind tunnel testing and are not contained
 in the static F-16 dataset used in this work \cite{vanoort2006}. The
 implementation presented in Section~7 therefore treats them as parameters to
 be varied rather than as known constants, and demonstrates that the
 qualitative hysteresis behaviour is present across the plausible range.
 
 
 \section{Model and Methodology}
 
 \subsection{Aerodynamic Dataset and Reference Quantities}
 
 All aerodynamic data used in this work originate from the wind tunnel
 programme documented in NASA Technical Paper 1538 \cite{nguyen1979}, in the
 digitised form compiled by van Oort and Sonneveldt \cite{vanoort2006}. The
 dataset comprises approximately forty-five tables covering the six force and
 moment coefficients as functions of angle of attack, sideslip angle and
 control surface deflection, together with dynamic derivative tables,
 leading-edge-flap increments and a deep-stall pitching moment increment. The
 angle-of-attack grid extends from $-20^{\circ}$ to $90^{\circ}$ at
 $5^{\circ}$ intervals up to $60^{\circ}$, with the remaining points at
 $70^{\circ}$, $80^{\circ}$ and $90^{\circ}$; the sideslip grid spans
 $\pm30^{\circ}$.
 
 The coefficients are non-dimensionalised using the reference quantities of
 the simulation model rather than the physical dimensions of the aircraft, and
 the distinction matters. The reference wing area is
 $S = 27.87\,\mathrm{m^{2}}$, the reference span is $b = 9.144\,\mathrm{m}$ and
 the mean aerodynamic chord is $\bar{c} = 3.450\,\mathrm{m}$
 \cite{nguyen1979,stevenslewis}. The reference span corresponds to
 $30\,\mathrm{ft}$ and is smaller than the physical span over the wingtip
 launchers; using the latter would introduce a systematic error of
 approximately three per cent in every rolling and yawing moment and in every
 roll and yaw damping term. Mass and inertia properties correspond to the
 nominal loading of the simulation model: $m = 9295.44\,\mathrm{kg}$,
 $I_{xx} = 12874.8$, $I_{yy} = 75673.6$, $I_{zz} = 85552.1$ and
 $I_{xz} = 1331.4\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{2}}$, with an engine angular momentum of
 $216.9\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{2}/s}$ about the longitudinal axis
 \cite{stevenslewis}. All tabulated moments are referenced to a centre of
 gravity at $0.35\bar{c}$.
 
 \subsection{Coefficient Buildup}
 
 The tabulated data cannot be used directly as coefficients; they must be
 assembled according to the formulation of the original model
 \cite{sonneveldt2006}. Three aspects of this assembly are structurally
 important and are easily misapplied.
 
 First, the leading-edge-flap tables are not increments. Each such table gives
 the total coefficient with the flap fully deployed, so the increment must be
 formed as the difference between the deployed-flap value and the clean value
 at zero stabilator deflection, and is then scaled linearly by
 $(1 - \delta_{\mathrm{lef}}/25)$. Treating these tables as increments
 directly produces a model that is incorrect at every flap setting including
 the retracted one.
 
 Second, the moment coefficients require centre-of-gravity transfer terms.
 Since the tables are referenced to $0.35\bar{c}$, any other centre of gravity
 requires
 \begin{equation}
 	C_{m} = C_{m,\mathrm{table}} + C_{Z}\left(x_{cg,\mathrm{ref}} - x_{cg}\right),
 	\qquad
 	C_{n} = C_{n,\mathrm{table}} - C_{Y}\left(x_{cg,\mathrm{ref}} - x_{cg}\right)\frac{\bar{c}}{b}.
 	\label{eq:cgtransfer}
 \end{equation}
 Omitting these terms restricts every stability result to the reference centre
 of gravity. This is not a minor restriction, since centre-of-gravity position
 is the parameter that governs the existence of the deep-stall equilibrium
 examined in Section~\ref{sec:static}.
 
 Third, the deep-stall pitching moment increment must be included. This term
 is zero below approximately $35^{\circ}$ angle of attack and becomes large
 above it, and without it the high-angle-of-attack equilibrium branch is
 displaced.
 
 Control surface effects are introduced as increments scaled linearly by
 deflection, using $\delta_{a}/20$ for the ailerons and $\delta_{r}/30$ for
 the rudder, consistent with the deflections at which the increment tables
 were measured. Dynamic terms are non-dimensionalised by $\bar{c}/2V$ for pitch
 rate and $b/2V$ for roll and yaw rate.
 
 Two simplifications are adopted and should be stated explicitly. The
 stabilator effectiveness factor $\eta(\delta_{h})$ of the original NASA
 formulation is not present in the digitised dataset and is therefore taken as
 unity. The leading-edge flap is held retracted throughout, rather than
 following its scheduled deflection. The second choice is deliberate: holding
 the flap fixed isolates the behaviour of the airframe from that of its
 control system, which is appropriate for a stability study of the bare
 aircraft.
 
 \subsection{Interpolation}
 
 The source model specifies linear interpolation, and linear interpolation is
 used wherever coefficient values are required. The choice is not neutral,
 however, and the reason is worth stating because it affects part of the
 analysis directly.
 
 Linear interpolation reproduces the tabulated values exactly and errs only
 between nodes. Comparison against cubic spline interpolation shows the
 largest disagreement anywhere in the dataset to be approximately $0.16$ in
 $C_{Z}$ at full nose-down stabilator, and approximately $0.009$ in $C_{m}$ at
 zero deflection, in both cases concentrated near $\alpha = 85^{\circ}$ where
 the grid is sparsest. For the purpose of evaluating coefficients, this is
 acceptable and the linear scheme is preferred because it matches the source.
 
 The situation is different for any calculation that differentiates the model.
 Linear interpolation produces a slope that is constant within each interval
 and discontinuous at every node, and eigenvalues of the linearised dynamics
 depend on that slope rather than on the coefficient value. Crossing the node
 at $\alpha = 10^{\circ}$ changes $\partial C_{m}/\partial\alpha$ by a factor
 of two and causes the computed growth rate to jump from $0.33$ to
 $1.12\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, with no corresponding change in the physics. for the eigenvalue
 analysis of Section 6 and the fold verification of Section 5.4, the
 angle-of-attack axis is therefore first refined to $0.25^{\circ}$ using
 monotone piecewise cubic interpolation, after which linear interpolation is
 applied on the refined grid. Coefficient values are essentially unchanged and
 the slope becomes effectively continuous.
 
 \subsection{Equations of Motion}
 
 The aircraft is modelled as a rigid body with eight states,
 \begin{equation}
 	\mathbf{x} = \left[\,V,\ \alpha,\ \beta,\ p,\ q,\ r,\ \phi,\ \theta\,\right]^{T},
 	\qquad
 	\mathbf{u} = \left[\,\delta_{t},\ \delta_{h},\ \delta_{a},\ \delta_{r}\,\right]^{T},
 	\label{eq:states}
 \end{equation}
 following the formulation used by Kolb \cite{kolb2017} for a comparable study
 of the F-18, so that the two sets of results are directly comparable.
 Altitude is treated as a fixed parameter rather than a state and is held at
 sea level throughout; heading is omitted because no term in the model depends
 on it. The rotational equations retain the $I_{xz}$ cross-product of inertia
 and the gyroscopic contribution of engine angular momentum. Engine spool
 dynamics are not modelled, so thrust follows the throttle command directly.
 
 Thrust is obtained from the idle, military and maximum tables of the original
 model, interpolated in Mach number and altitude and blended by commanded
 power level \cite{nguyen1979,stevenslewis}. Atmospheric properties follow the
 International Standard Atmosphere.
 
 \subsection{Trim and Equilibrium Families}
 
 Trim requires the simultaneous solution of three conditions: force balance
 along the flight path, force balance normal to it, and moment balance about
 the centre of gravity. Solving the moment condition alone, that is, locating
 the zeros of $C_{m}(\alpha)$, yields angles of attack at which the pitching
 moment vanishes but at which the aircraft is not in equilibrium, since
 nothing constrains lift to balance weight.
 
 Two distinct equilibrium families are used in this work. The first fixes
 airspeed and flight path angle and solves for throttle, stabilator and angle
 of attack; this is conventional trim and is used for verification. The second
 fixes throttle and allows the flight path angle to float, solving for
 stabilator, angle of attack and flight path angle. The second family is
 necessary because level flight cannot reach high angle of attack: sustaining
 altitude above approximately $7^{\circ}$ at sea level requires an airspeed
 below that at which the aircraft can support its own weight. Allowing the
 aircraft to descend extends the family to $\alpha = 40^{\circ}$ and beyond, at
 flight path angles reaching $-37^{\circ}$. These remain genuine equilibria of
 the full system, with all angular rates, sideslip and bank angle zero.
 
 \subsection{Verification}
 
 The implementation is verified against the standard published trim case for
 straight and level flight at $152.4\,\mathrm{m/s}$ ($500\,\mathrm{ft/s}$) at
 sea level with the centre of gravity at $0.35\bar{c}$. The solver returns
 $\alpha = 2.006^{\circ}$, $\delta_{h} = -0.209^{\circ}$ and a throttle setting
 of $0.128$, with a numerical residual of ($9.9\times10^{-13}$). The published F-16 trim data give values close to ($\alpha=2.11^\circ$), ($\delta_e=-0.76^\circ$), and ($\delta_t=0.14$) for a comparable condition \cite{stevenslewis}.
 
 The differences arise from the modifications introduced in the present reduced-order implementation, including the use of a fixed stabilator effectiveness factor, the retracted leading-edge flap assumption, and the simplified propulsion model. Therefore, the comparison is used to assess consistency of the implemented equilibrium formulation rather than to claim exact reproduction of the original reference model. The very small residual confirms numerical convergence of the present trim solution.
 
 Linearisation about this point yields the eigenvalues
 $-3.60$, $-0.40 \pm 2.85\mathrm{i}$, $-0.019 \pm 0.123\mathrm{i}$, $-0.016$,
 $+0.571$ and $-2.671\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, corresponding respectively to roll
 subsidence, dutch roll, phugoid, spiral, and a pitch mode which has split
 into two real roots rather than forming an oscillatory short period. The positive real root, with a time to double of approximately \(1.2\,\mathrm{s}\), indicates an unstable longitudinal mode of the bare airframe, consistent with the relaxed longitudinal static stability investigated in the original NASA study \cite{nguyen1979}.
 
 
 
 \section{Static Aerodynamic Characteristics and Equilibria}
 \label{sec:static}
 
 
 \subsection{Lift and Drag}
 
 The dataset provides body-axis force coefficients only. Lift and drag are
 wind-axis quantities and are obtained by rotating the body-axis force through
 the angle of attack. At zero sideslip,
 \begin{equation}
 	C_{L} = C_{X}\sin\alpha - C_{Z}\cos\alpha,
 	\qquad
 	C_{D} = -C_{X}\cos\alpha - C_{Z}\sin\alpha .
 	\label{eq:windaxes}
 \end{equation}
 The transformation was verified against the converged trim solution of
 Section~\ref{sec:verification}, which is computed entirely in body axes and
 never uses $C_{L}$ or $C_{D}$. At that condition the lift obtained from
 Equation~\eqref{eq:windaxes}, together with the vertical component of thrust,
 balances the aircraft weight to $91\,157.1\,\mathrm{N}$ on both sides, and the
 drag balances the horizontal component of thrust to $8604.9\,\mathrm{N}$ on
 both sides. The agreement is exact to the precision of the solver.
 

 Figure~5.1 shows the result. The lift curve follows its low-incidence linear
 extrapolation, of slope $0.067$ per degree fitted between $0^{\circ}$ and
 $10^{\circ}$, closely up to approximately $10^{\circ}$, and departs from it
 progressively thereafter. The maximum lift coefficient of $1.72$ occurs at
 $\alpha = 36^{\circ}$, well beyond the angle at which the outer wing panels
 stall. This is consistent with the wind tunnel flow-visualisation results
 discussed in Section~2, in which the outer panels were observed to stall near
 $\alpha = 20^{\circ}$ while the wing-body strake continued to generate lift to
 a maximum near $35^{\circ}$ \cite{nguyen1979}. The present model, assembled
 independently from the tabulated data, agrees with that reported angle to
 within one degree.
 
 The drag coefficient falls to a minimum near zero incidence and rises
 monotonically thereafter across the remainder of the range. It does not follow
 the classical parabolic polar $C_{D} = C_{D_{0}} + kC_{L}^{2}$: beyond
 $36^{\circ}$ the lift coefficient falls while the drag continues to increase,
 a combination the parabolic form cannot represent at all. The lift-to-drag
 ratio reaches a maximum of approximately $11.6$ near $\alpha = 3^{\circ}$ and
 falls to approximately unity by $45^{\circ}$.
 
 It is worth noting that the contribution of thrust to the vertical force
 balance is negligible at low incidence, amounting to some $0.3$ per cent of
 the weight at the trim condition examined above, but is not negligible at high
 angle of attack, where the thrust vector is inclined substantially relative to
 the flight path.
 
 \subsection{Pitching Moment and Control Effectiveness}
 
 
 \begin{figure}[htbp]
 	\centering
 	\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Cm_plots.png}
 	\caption{Pitching moment coefficient against angle of attack. Upper panel:
 		baseline at zero sideslip and zero stabilator deflection. Centre panel:
 		effect of stabilator deflection across its full range. Lower panel: effect
 		of sideslip.}
 	\label{fig:cm}
 \end{figure}
 
 Figure~\ref{fig:cm} shows the pitching moment coefficient. Three features
 distinguish it from the behaviour assumed by linear stability theory.
 
 The slope $\partial C_{m}/\partial\alpha$ changes sign more than once across
 the range, so the aircraft is neither uniformly stable nor uniformly unstable
 in pitch; static stability is a property of the flight condition rather than
 of the airframe alone. The stabilator increments are not uniform with angle of
 attack, so control effectiveness itself varies with flight condition, and in
 particular the nose-down authority available at high incidence is not the same
 as that available in cruise. The response to sideslip is not symmetric in the
 manner a linear model requires.
 
 The consequence for equilibrium is direct. In a linear model there is exactly
 one angle of attack at which the pitching moment vanishes for each stabilator
 setting. Here there may be none, one, or several, and which case applies
 depends on both the stabilator deflection and the centre-of-gravity position.

 Figure~\ref{fig:pitcheq} presents the angles of attack at which the assembled
 pitching moment vanishes, obtained over a sweep of stabilator deflection at
 three centre-of-gravity positions, with each equilibrium classified by the
 sign of $\partial C_{m}/\partial\alpha$ evaluated at the root.
 
 Two results follow. First, at the reference centre of gravity of
 $0.35\bar{c}$ the low-incidence equilibrium is statically unstable. This is
 not an artefact but the intended characteristic of the aircraft, which is
 flown with relaxed longitudinal static stability and depends on its flight
 control system for pitch stabilisation \cite{nguyen1979}.
 
Second, a statically stable equilibrium exists at high angle of attack with the stabilator at its full nose-down limit. At this condition, the available nose-down control authority is fully applied, yet the aircraft retains a stable high-incidence equilibrium. The equilibrium analysis therefore indicates a deep-stall-like state from which the stabilator alone cannot eliminate the high-incidence equilibrium. The predicted equilibrium occurs above \(70^\circ\), and this value should not be interpreted as a quantitative prediction of the physical F-16 deep-stall angle, as discussed in Section 8.

 
 The same figure shows that this behaviour is absent at $0.30\bar{c}$, where
 the high-incidence stable branch exists only for nose-up deflections and
 nose-down control therefore always recovers the aircraft. The transition
 between the two situations is examined next.
 
 \subsection{Continuation in Centre-of-Gravity Position}
 
 
 \begin{figure}[htbp]
 	\centering
 	\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Cg_bifurcation.png}
 	\caption{Equilibrium angles of attack at full nose-down stabilator
 		($\delta_{h} = +25^{\circ}$) as a function of centre-of-gravity position.
 		The dashed line marks the critical position at which a stable and an
 		unstable equilibrium appear together.}
 	\label{fig:cgbif}
 \end{figure}
 
 Holding the stabilator at its full nose-down limit and treating the centre of
 gravity as the varied parameter isolates the onset of the deep stall.
 Figure~\ref{fig:cgbif} shows the result. Forward of a critical position no
 equilibrium exists at high angle of attack. Aft of it, a stable and an
 unstable equilibrium appear simultaneously and separate as the centre of
 gravity moves further aft. The critical position, located by bisection, is
 $x_{cg} = 0.3071\bar{c}$.
 
 
 The appearance of two equilibria as a parameter crosses a threshold is
 consistent with a saddle-node bifurcation, but does not by itself establish
 one. A discontinuity in the interpolated model could produce the same
 appearance without any smooth fold being present, and this concern is not
 hypothetical here: with linear interpolation on the raw grid the two
 equilibria merge at $\alpha = 70^{\circ}$, which is itself a table node. Two
 further conditions were therefore tested, both using the smoothed model
 described in Section~4.3.
 
 The first is tangency. At a saddle-node the two equilibria merge with a common
 tangent, so both $C_{m} = 0$ and $\partial C_{m}/\partial\alpha = 0$ hold at
 the critical point, and the slopes at the two roots must fall towards zero as
 the parameter approaches its critical value. The second is scaling. The normal
 form of a saddle-node gives a separation between the two roots proportional to
 the square root of the distance from the critical parameter value, an exponent
 no interpolation artefact reproduces. Table~\ref{tab:fold} reports both.

 	\label{tab:fold}

 Both slopes decrease towards zero as the critical position is approached, by
 factors of approximately five and eight respectively over the range examined,
 which is consistent with the required tangency. The separation follows a
 power law in the distance from the critical position with a fitted exponent of
 $0.56$, against the theoretical value of $0.5$; the agreement is shown in
 Figure~\ref{fig:foldscaling}. The small excess over $0.5$ is attributable to
 the finite range of the fit and to the outermost points lying too far from the
 critical value for the normal form to apply. It is far removed from the
 exponent of unity that a piecewise-linear corner would produce.
 
 \begin{figure}[htbp]
 	\centering
 	\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{root_seperation}
 	\caption{Separation between the two high-incidence equilibria against
 		distance from the critical centre-of-gravity position, with a square root
 		reference shown dashed.}
 	\label{fig:foldscaling}
 \end{figure}
 
 The critical position is $0.3071\bar{c}$ under both the linear and the
 smoothed interpolation schemes. That the two schemes agree, while only the
 smoothed one permits the tangency to be resolved, indicates that the
 transition is a property of the aerodynamic data rather than of the numerical
 representation.
 
 
 The transition is therefore a saddle-node, or fold, bifurcation. Its
 significance here is qualitative: the deep-stall-like state does not become
 gradually more probable as the centre of gravity moves aft. It does not exist
 below the threshold and it exists above it.
 
 A second and more practically relevant trend appears in the same figure. The
 unstable branch forms the boundary between the set of states that return to
 normal flight and the set that converge to the deep stall. That branch
 descends steadily as the centre of gravity moves aft, from approximately
 $69^{\circ}$ at $0.31\bar{c}$ to approximately $42^{\circ}$ at
 $0.375\bar{c}$. Crossing the threshold therefore creates the deep-stall state,
 while moving further aft progressively reduces the disturbance required to
 reach it. The two effects are distinct, and only the first is a bifurcation.
 
 
 \section{Dynamic Stability}
 \label{sec:dynamic}
 
 \subsection{Method}
 
 Static stability determines whether the pitching moment opposes a change in
 angle of attack. It cannot determine whether a disturbance oscillates, how
 rapidly it grows or decays, or whether longitudinal and lateral motion
 interact. These require the eigenvalues of the linearised dynamics.
 
 The eight-state model of Equation~\eqref{eq:states} is linearised numerically
 about each member of the descending equilibrium family described in
 \ref{Section:4.5}, and the eigenvalues of the resulting Jacobian are computed. Modes
 are identified from the participation of each state in the corresponding
 eigenvector, following the approach used by Kolb \cite{kolb2017}, rather than
 by sorting eigenvalues by magnitude. Sorting by magnitude relabels modes
 whenever two of them exchange frequency, which both fragments the resulting
 curves and produces spurious apparent bifurcations.
 
 \subsection{Three Regimes of Stability}
 
 % ---------------------------------------------------------------------
 % FIGURE: run run_modal_analysis.m, tab "Growth rate".
 % This is the single most important figure in the report.
 % ---------------------------------------------------------------------
 \begin{figure}[htbp]
 	\centering
 	\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{growth_rate.png}
 	\caption{Largest real part of the eigenvalues along the equilibrium family,
 		coloured according to whether the least stable mode belongs to the
 		longitudinal or the lateral-directional family. Values above zero indicate
 		a divergent bare airframe.}
 	\label{fig:growth}
 \end{figure}
 
 Figure~\ref{fig:growth} shows the largest real part of the eigenvalue set as a
 function of trim angle of attack. Three distinct regimes are apparent.
 
 Below approximately $15^{\circ}$ the aircraft is unstable, and the unstable
 mode is longitudinal. This is the relaxed static stability already identified
 in Section~\ref{sec:static}, now expressed as a growth rate rather than a
 slope sign.
 
 Between approximately $15^{\circ}$ and $31^{\circ}$ the bare airframe is
 stable. Every eigenvalue lies in the left half plane, and no augmentation is
 required for stability in this band.
 
 Above approximately $32^{\circ}$ the aircraft is unstable again, but the
 unstable mode is now lateral-directional. The change of family is important:
 the aircraft does not simply become unstable once more, it becomes unstable by
 a different mechanism in a different axis. The two mechanisms are examined
 separately below.
 
 \subsection{Dutch-Roll Instability and Hopf-Type Stability Boundary}
 
 % ---------------------------------------------------------------------
 % FIGURE: run run_modal_analysis.m, tab "Damping".
 % ---------------------------------------------------------------------
 \begin{figure}[htbp]
 	\centering
 	\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{damping.png}
 	\caption{Damping ratio of the oscillatory modes, identified by eigenvector
 		participation. A crossing below zero indicates a complex conjugate pair
 		entering the right half plane, that is, a Hopf bifurcation.}
 	\label{fig:damping}
 \end{figure}
 
 Figure~\ref{fig:damping} shows the damping ratios of the oscillatory modes.
 The dutch roll damping decreases steadily from approximately $0.31$ at
 $\alpha = 20^{\circ}$ and crosses zero near $\alpha = 32^{\circ}$, at a
 frequency of approximately $1.1\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ corresponding to a period of
 about $5.6\,\mathrm{s}$.
 
 At approximately \(\alpha=32\text{--}33^\circ\), the Dutch-roll eigenvalue pair approaches and crosses the imaginary axis, indicating the onset of lateral-directional dynamic instability. This eigenvalue crossing is consistent with a Hopf-type stability boundary. However, because the present study does not calculate the first Lyapunov coefficient or perform a nonlinear time-domain simulation demonstrating a finite-amplitude periodic solution, a complete nonlinear Hopf bifurcation cannot be established from the present analysis alone.
 
 
 A second feature of Figure~\ref{fig:damping} is worth noting. Above
 approximately $22.5^{\circ}$ the roll subsidence and spiral modes, both real
 roots at lower angles of attack, coalesce into a slow and heavily damped
 oscillation. Mode coalescence of this kind is itself a nonlinear effect with
 no counterpart in a fixed-structure linear model.
 
 \subsection{Directional Divergence}
 
 \begin{figure}[htbp]
 	\centering
 	\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{real_roots.png}
 	\caption{Real parts of the non-oscillatory modes. A crossing above zero
 		indicates a divergence rather than a growing oscillation.}
 	\label{fig:realroots}
 \end{figure}
 
 Beyond the Hopf bifurcation a second and more severe instability appears. Near
 $\alpha = 34^{\circ}$ a real eigenvalue crosses into the right half plane,
 with an eigenvector dominated by sideslip angle and yaw rate. This is not an
 oscillation but a directional divergence, in which sideslip grows
 monotonically. It corresponds to the departure mode commonly termed nose
 slice.
 
 Figure~\ref{fig:realroots} also shows the low-angle-of-attack behaviour of the
 pitch mode. Below approximately $15^{\circ}$ it consists of two real roots
 rather than an oscillatory short period, one divergent and one convergent. The
 divergent root is the relaxed static stability of the airframe; the short
 period only becomes oscillatory above $15^{\circ}$, which is consistent with
 its appearance in Figure~\ref{fig:damping} at the same angle.
 
 \subsection{Consistency with the Classical Departure Criterion}
 
 
 \begin{figure}[htbp]
 	\centering
 	\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{departure.png}
 	\caption{Dynamic directional stability parameter
 		$C_{n\beta,\mathrm{dyn}}$ against angle of attack. Negative values indicate
 		susceptibility to directional divergence.}
 	\label{fig:departure}
 \end{figure}
 
 The classical departure criterion for directional divergence is the dynamic
 directional stability parameter
 \begin{equation}
 	C_{n\beta,\mathrm{dyn}} = C_{n\beta}\cos\alpha
 	- \frac{I_{zz}}{I_{xx}}\,C_{l\beta}\sin\alpha,
 	\label{eq:cnbdyn}
 \end{equation}
 which predicts departure where it becomes negative. Evaluated from the static
 coefficients, Equation~\eqref{eq:cnbdyn} crosses zero at
 $\alpha = 34.0^{\circ}$, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:departure}.
 
 The eigenvalue analysis places the directional divergence at
 $\alpha = 33.9^{\circ}$. These two predictions are obtained by entirely
 independent routes: the first from two static derivatives and an inertia
 ratio, with no dynamics involved; the second from the eigenvalues of a
 linearised eight-state model at a trimmed equilibrium. Their agreement to
 within $0.1^{\circ}$ provides mutual validation of the model implementation
 and of the criterion.
 
 It should be noted that $C_{n\beta,\mathrm{dyn}}$ is negative only over a
 bounded interval, approximately $34^{\circ}$ to $39^{\circ}$, rather than
 remaining negative at all higher angles.
  
  The eigenvalue analysis of Section~6.4 places the directional divergence at
  $\alpha = 33.9^{\circ}$, and the two results therefore differ by
  $0.1^{\circ}$. This agreement should be interpreted carefully. The two
  calculations are computationally distinct: the first evaluates a closed-form
  expression in two static derivatives and an inertia ratio, while the second
  extracts an eigenvalue from the numerically linearised eight-state model at a
  trimmed equilibrium. They are not, however, independent. Both draw on the same
  aerodynamic database and the same mass properties, and
  $C_{n\beta,\mathrm{dyn}}$ is itself a reduced-order approximation to the
  directional root whose sign change the eigenvalue analysis detects, obtained
  by retaining only the static sideslip derivatives and neglecting the damping
  terms and the coupling to the longitudinal states.
  
  What the comparison establishes is therefore internal consistency rather than
  external validation. The agreement to $0.1^{\circ}$ confirms that the
  lateral-directional part of the linearisation has been implemented correctly,
  in the sense that the full model reproduces the behaviour of the standard
  reduced-order criterion in the regime where that criterion is expected to
  apply. It does not constitute independent confirmation that the physical
  departure boundary of the aircraft lies at $34^{\circ}$; both results would be
  displaced together by any error in the underlying aerodynamic data or in the
  assumed inertias.
  
  It should be noted that $C_{n\beta,\mathrm{dyn}}$ is negative only over a
  bounded interval, approximately $34^{\circ}$ to $39^{\circ}$, rather than
  remaining negative at all higher angles. The criterion therefore identifies a
  departure-susceptible band rather than an upper limit on angle of attack.
  
 
 \section{Unsteady Aerodynamic Modelling}
 \label{sec:unsteady}
 
 \subsection{Recovering the Separation State}

 % FIGURE: run run_goman_khrabrov.m, tab "Separation point".
 % Optional. Include if you have room; the hysteresis figure matters more.
 % ---------------------------------------------------------------------
 \begin{figure}[htbp]
 	\centering
 	\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{seperation_point.png}
 	\caption{Steady separation point position $x_{0}$ recovered from the
 		tabulated static normal force by inversion of the Kirchhoff relation.}
 	\label{fig:sep}
 \end{figure}
 
 The state-space formulation of Equation~\eqref{eq:goman} requires the steady
 separation characteristic $x_{0}(\alpha)$. This is obtained from the static
 dataset itself by inverting the Kirchhoff relation for the normal force, with
 the attached-flow slope fitted over the range $4^{\circ}$ to $12^{\circ}$
 giving $C_{N\alpha} \approx 4.03$ per radian. The resulting curve, shown in
 Figure~\ref{fig:sep}, is unity below approximately $17^{\circ}$, indicating
 fully attached flow, and decreases monotonically thereafter, reaching
 approximately $0.87$ at $30^{\circ}$ and $0.58$ at $45^{\circ}$. The model is
 therefore meaningful only above the onset of separation; below it there is no
 separated flow to lag.
 
 \subsection{Aerodynamic Hysteresis}
 
 % ---------------------------------------------------------------------
 % FIGURE: run run_goman_khrabrov.m, tab "Hysteresis loops".
 % This is the key figure for this section.
 % ---------------------------------------------------------------------
 \begin{figure}[htbp]
 	\centering
 	\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{hysteresis_loop.png}
 	\caption{Pitching moment against angle of attack for a prescribed
 		sinusoidal oscillation about $30^{\circ}$ with an amplitude of
 		$8^{\circ}$. The static table traces a single curve; the unsteady model

 	\label{fig:hyst}
 \end{figure}
 
 Figure~\ref{fig:hyst} shows the response of the model to a prescribed
 sinusoidal variation in angle of attack. The static table produces a single
 curve, traced identically in both directions. The unsteady model produces a
 closed loop: at $\alpha = 30^{\circ}$ and an oscillation frequency of
 $0.5\,\mathrm{Hz}$, the pitching moment coefficient is approximately $-0.002$
 while the angle of attack is increasing and $-0.055$ while it is decreasing.
 The instantaneous angle of attack is identical in the two cases and the
 aerodynamic moment differs by an order of magnitude.
 
 This is the behaviour that no static lookup table can represent, and it is the
 quantitative justification for the argument advanced in Section~3.
 
 \subsection{Sensitivity to the Model Parameters}
 
 % ---------------------------------------------------------------------
 % FIGURE: run run_goman_khrabrov.m, tab "Parameter sweep".
 % ---------------------------------------------------------------------
 \begin{figure}[htbp]
 	\centering
 	\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{parameter_swept.png}
 	\caption{Width of the pitching moment hysteresis loop against oscillation
 		frequency, for four values of the relaxation time constant $\tau_{1}$.}
 	\label{fig:gksweep}
 \end{figure}
 
 The time constants $\tau_{1}$ and $\tau_{2}$ are determined from
 forced-oscillation testing and are not available in the static dataset. Rather
 than adopting values from another aircraft, both are treated as parameters and
 varied. Figure~\ref{fig:gksweep} shows the resulting loop width as a function
 of oscillation frequency.
 
 The loop width exhibits a maximum at an intermediate frequency for every value
 of $\tau_{1}$ examined. The interpretation is physical: at low frequency the
 separation point follows its steady value closely and there is negligible lag;
 at high frequency it cannot respond at all and remains effectively frozen;
 maximum hysteresis occurs at intermediate frequencies where the flow is
 responding but failing to keep pace. Since this behaviour is present across
 the plausible parameter range, the qualitative conclusion does not depend on
 the particular values chosen, although the magnitude of the effect does.
 
 
 \section{Limitations}
 \label{sec:limitations}
 
 \subsection{Completeness of the Aerodynamic Model}
 
 Two components of the original NASA formulation are absent from the digitised
 dataset and are therefore not represented. The stabilator effectiveness factor
 $\eta(\delta_{h})$, which reduces the tabulated pitching moment increment at
 large deflections, is taken as unity throughout. Since the deep-stall results
 of Section~\ref{sec:static} depend on the pitching moment available at full
 nose-down deflection, this is the omission most likely to affect them, and it
 would act in the direction of overstating the available nose-down authority.
 The speedbrake contribution is likewise absent, but is not required for any of
 the analyses presented.
 
 The leading-edge flap is held retracted rather than following its scheduled
 deflection. This is a deliberate choice rather than an omission: holding the
 flap fixed isolates the behaviour of the airframe from that of its control
 system, which is appropriate for a study of bare-airframe stability. It does
 mean, however, that the results describe the aircraft with its high-lift
 devices inoperative, which is not the configuration in which it is normally
 flown.
 
 The engine thrust tables were transcribed from the reference rather than
 verified against a primary document. Trim throttle setting depends on them
 directly. The trim angle of attack, trim stabilator deflection, static
 stability, eigenvalues and bifurcation results are essentially insensitive to
 thrust, so the principal findings of this work do not rest on these values.
 
 \subsection{Resolution of the Tabulated Data}
 
 The angle-of-attack grid is spaced at $5^{\circ}$ intervals to $60^{\circ}$
 and then contains only three further points, at $70^{\circ}$, $80^{\circ}$ and
 $90^{\circ}$. All behaviour reported above $60^{\circ}$ therefore rests on a
 very sparse sampling of the underlying wind tunnel data, and any structure
 appearing between those points is a property of the interpolation rather than
 of the measurements.
 
 This bears directly on the deep-stall result. The present model places the
 stable high-incidence equilibrium above $70^{\circ}$ angle of attack, whereas
 the deep stall of the F-16 is generally reported in the range $55^{\circ}$ to
 $60^{\circ}$. Three factors plausibly contribute: the sparse grid in precisely
 this region, the omission of $\eta(\delta_{h})$ noted above, and the retracted
 leading-edge flap. The qualitative result, that a statically stable
 equilibrium exists at high incidence under full nose-down control at the
 reference centre of gravity, is robust to all three. The particular angle at
 which it occurs is not, and should not be quoted as a prediction.
 
 A comparable caution applies to features of the eigenvalue results that appear
 between widely spaced nodes. The local maximum in growth rate near
 $\alpha = 12.5^{\circ}$, for example, lies between table points at
 $10^{\circ}$ and $15^{\circ}$; the existence of a transition across that
 interval is supported by the data, but its shape and peak magnitude are not.
 
 
 \subsection{Interpolation and the Absence of Continuation}
 
 Linear interpolation reproduces the tabulated values and matches the source
 model, but yields a slope that is discontinuous at every node. This has two
 consequences for the present work.
 
 The first concerns the eigenvalue results. Eigenvalues computed from a
 linearly interpolated model jump at table nodes for no physical reason, as
 described in Section~4.3. Refining the angle-of-attack axis before
 interpolating removes this behaviour, but it introduces an assumption that the
 original dataset does not support: between nodes, the refined model asserts a
 curvature that was never measured. All eigenvalues reported in Section~6, and
 the tangency test of Section~5.4, are therefore properties of the smoothed
 numerical representation rather than of the measured data directly. This
 matters particularly for the transitions identified near $\alpha = 15^{\circ}$,
 $32^{\circ}$ and $34^{\circ}$, none of which coincides with a table point, and
 all of which are consequently located using interpolated slopes.
 
 The second concerns the method used to trace equilibrium branches. The results
 of Sections~5.3, 5.4 and~6 are obtained by parameter sweeps: the varied
 parameter is stepped, the equilibrium condition is solved independently at
 each step, and in Section~6 each solution is used to seed the next. This is
 adequate for the equilibrium families examined, whose branches are widely
 separated and single-valued in the swept parameter over the ranges considered.
 It is not equivalent to a continuation method, and it has a specific
 limitation: because the parameter is prescribed at each step, the procedure
 cannot follow a branch that turns back on itself, and would fail to trace an
 equilibrium curve through a fold rather than merely detect one.
 
 Pseudo-arclength continuation, which treats the parameter as a further unknown
 and steps along the solution curve by arclength, is the standard remedy and is
 the method used by Kolb~\cite{kolb2017}. It was not applied here. Exploratory
 calculations indicated that the Newton corrector on which such methods depend
 converges poorly against the present model, because the corrector requires a
 continuous Jacobian and the tabulated aerodynamic model does not provide one.
 Kolb does not encounter this difficulty because the F-18 model used in that
 work is given in analytic form. The established approach for tabulated data is
 to fit the tables to smooth global functions before applying continuation,
 which is one of the motivations for the global nonlinear modelling techniques
 discussed in Section~3~\cite{ismail2023}. That fitting was not carried out in
 the present work, and its absence is the principal reason the bifurcation
 analysis here is restricted to detecting and characterising transitions rather
 than to tracing complete solution branches.
 
 \subsection{Scope of the Dynamic Analysis}
 
 The equilibria examined in Section~\ref{sec:dynamic} have zero sideslip, zero
 bank angle and zero angular rates. Spin is an equilibrium with non-zero roll
 and yaw rates and is therefore outside the set of solutions considered here.
 The departure boundary identified at $\alpha \approx 34^{\circ}$ indicates the
 condition at which the aircraft ceases to hold wings-level flight; it does not
 describe the motion that follows, nor whether that motion terminates in a
 developed spin.
 
 The analysis is also restricted to the identification of bifurcation points
 rather than their classification. The Hopf bifurcation of the dutch roll mode
 reported in Section~\ref{sec:dynamic} is located by the crossing of a complex
 conjugate pair, but no first Lyapunov coefficient was computed, so it is not
 established whether the resulting limit cycle appears on the stable or the
 unstable side of the bifurcation. That distinction determines whether the
 oscillation grows to a bounded amplitude from which recovery is possible, and
 it would require dedicated continuation software of the kind used in
 \cite{kolb2017}.
 
 No time-domain simulation was performed. The stability results presented are
 therefore predictions of the linearised dynamics about equilibrium, and have
 not been confirmed by integrating the nonlinear equations from a perturbed
 initial condition.
 
 \subsection{The Unsteady Model}
 
 The time constants $\tau_{1}$ and $\tau_{2}$ of the state-space formulation
 are properties of the flow and are obtained from forced-oscillation wind
 tunnel testing, which the static dataset does not include. The sensitivity
 study of Section~\ref{sec:unsteady} establishes that hysteresis is present
 across the plausible range of these parameters and that its dependence on
 frequency has a consistent form, but the magnitude of the effect for the F-16
 specifically cannot be determined from the available data.
 
 The implementation also adopts a simplified reconstruction, in which the
 coefficient is recovered by evaluating the static tables at the angle of
 attack corresponding to the current separation state, rather than through the
 Kirchhoff expression used in the original formulation \cite{goman1994}. Both
 reduce exactly to the static model in steady flight, and both produce
 hysteresis under unsteady conditions, but they are not identical.
 
 Finally, the unsteady model is exercised in isolation, driven by a prescribed
 variation in angle of attack. It is not coupled to the equations of motion, so
 the effect of unsteady aerodynamics on the stability boundaries reported in
 Section~\ref{sec:dynamic} has not been assessed. Since those boundaries lie at
 angles of attack where the flow is separated, this coupling is the most
 significant extension the present work would admit.
 
 
 \section{Conclusions}
 
 This project set out to determine how nonlinear aerodynamic effects at high
 angles of attack influence the stability and manoeuvrability of a
 high-performance fighter aircraft, using the F-16 as a case study. A complete
 nonlinear aerodynamic model was assembled from the NASA Technical Paper 1538
 dataset, embedded in a six-degree-of-freedom simulation, and analysed by
 equilibrium, bifurcation and eigenvalue methods.
 
 \subsection{Principal Findings}
 
 The static aerodynamic coefficients are nonlinear in a way that changes the
 structure of the problem rather than merely its numerical values. The
 lift curve departs from its linear extrapolation beyond approximately
 $10^{\circ}$ and reaches a maximum near $36^{\circ}$, well past the stall of
 the outer wing panels, reproducing the behaviour attributed in the source
 literature to the wing-body strake vortex. The pitching moment slope changes
 sign more than once across the angle-of-attack range, so the number of
 equilibrium states is not fixed: depending on stabilator deflection and centre
 of gravity there may be none, one, or several.
 
 This variability has a direct consequence for controllability. At the
 reference centre of gravity the aircraft possesses a statically stable
 equilibrium at high angle of attack with the stabilator at its full nose-down
 limit, a state from which the stabilator alone cannot recover it. Continuation
 in centre-of-gravity position shows that this deep-stall state is created at
 $x_{cg} = 0.3071\bar{c}$ through a saddle-node bifurcation, appearing together
 with an unstable equilibrium that forms the boundary of its basin of
 attraction. The state does not exist forward of that position. Moving further
 aft does not deepen it but lowers the boundary, from approximately
 $69^{\circ}$ at $0.31\bar{c}$ to approximately $42^{\circ}$ at
 $0.375\bar{c}$, progressively reducing the disturbance required to enter it.
 
 Eigenvalue analysis along a descending equilibrium family identifies three
 distinct regimes of dynamic stability. Below approximately $15^{\circ}$ the
 bare airframe is divergent in pitch, which is the relaxed longitudinal static
 stability for which the aircraft was designed. Between approximately
 $15^{\circ}$ and $31^{\circ}$ it is stable without augmentation. Above
 approximately $32^{\circ}$ it is unstable again, but in a different axis and
 by a different mechanism: the dutch roll mode crosses into negative damping in
 a Hopf bifurcation, corresponding physically to wing rock, and is followed
 near $34^{\circ}$ by a directional divergence in sideslip and yaw rate
 corresponding to nose slice departure.
 
 The departure boundary is identified consistently by two computationally
 distinct evaluations of the same model. The classical criterion
 $C_nbeta$,$dyn$, a closed-form expression in two static derivatives and an
 inertia ratio, changes sign at alpha = 34.0 degrees; the eigenvalue
 analysis of the linearised eight-state model places the divergence at
 alpha = 33.9 degrees. Since the criterion is a reduced-order
 approximation to the same eigenvalue, this agreement confirms the
 internal consistency of the implementation rather than validating the
 result against independent evidence.
 
 Finally, implementation of the Goman--Khrabrov state-space formulation
 confirms that the static representation underlying all of the above becomes
 quantitatively inadequate once the angle of attack is changing. Under a
 prescribed oscillation about $30^{\circ}$ at $0.5\,\mathrm{Hz}$, the pitching
 moment coefficient differs by roughly an order of magnitude between the
 increasing and decreasing branches at the same instantaneous angle of attack.
 
 \subsection{Implications for High-Manoeuvrability Design}
 
 Taken together, these results describe an aircraft whose useful envelope is
 bounded not by a single limit but by several distinct mechanisms, each
 arising from a different feature of the nonlinear aerodynamics. The pitch
 divergence at low incidence is a designed characteristic, traded against
 manoeuvrability and rendered acceptable by a full-authority flight control
 system. The wing rock and directional divergence at high incidence are not
 designed but tolerated, and they define the practical upper boundary of the
 envelope. The deep stall is neither, and represents a region of the state
 space that must be avoided rather than managed.
 
 The design responses adopted in subsequent aircraft follow directly from this
 structure. Relaxed static stability, which appears here as the positive real
 eigenvalue below $15^{\circ}$, has become standard practice rather than an
 exception, because the manoeuvrability it purchases is available whenever the
 control system is. Thrust vectoring addresses a different limitation, namely
 that the deep stall and the departure boundary both arise where aerodynamic
 control surfaces lose effectiveness; a control moment that does not depend on
 dynamic pressure or on attached flow removes the mechanism by which those
 states become inescapable. The F-16 itself illustrates the alternative
 approach of accepting the aerodynamic limitation and mitigating it
 operationally, through centre-of-gravity management and a manual pitch
 override provision for deep-stall recovery.
 
 \subsection{Recommendations for Further Work}
 
 Three extensions follow naturally from the limitations identified in
 Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. Fitting the tabulated aerodynamic data to
 smooth global functions would remove the interpolation discontinuity that prevented continuation methods from being applied, and would permit the equilibrium branches to
 be followed around folds using established continuation software. Extending
 the equilibrium search to include non-zero angular rates would allow steady
 spin states to be located, which the present formulation excludes by
 construction. Coupling the unsteady state-space model to the equations of
 motion, rather than exercising it in isolation, would establish whether the
 stability boundaries reported here are displaced once the finite response time
 of the separated flow is accounted for. The last of these is the most
 significant, since both high-incidence instabilities occur in precisely the
 regime where the static assumption is least justified.
